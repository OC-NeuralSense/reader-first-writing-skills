# Revision and the Fidelity Invariant

Revision is where diagnosis becomes change. A diagnostic pass found and located faults; revision repairs them. But repair is bounded by a rule that overrides every other consideration in this method: **a revision must preserve meaning.** A rewrite that reads better but says something different is not an improvement. It is a defect wearing the costume of one.

This chapter separates the two kinds of revision, states the fidelity invariant in full, explains why an unresolved same-passage conflict is escalated rather than decided, and describes how to compare an original against a revision to catch meaning that quietly slipped.

## Two revision moves, kept distinct

Just as diagnosis works on two layers, revision has two distinct moves, and they should not be run as one blurred pass.

**Structural revision** repairs the architecture. Its operations are re-grouping (moving a claim under the parent it actually belongs to), re-ordering (sequencing a group by a single consistent principle), re-summarizing (rewriting a parent so it honestly captures its children), and cutting (removing a claim that earns no place). Structural revision changes what sits where and what stands over what. It does not, by itself, polish sentences.

**Prose revision** repairs the surface. Its operations are reordering the parts of a sentence, fixing coherence so the reader can recover the intended relation, resolving ambiguous references, turning abstract noun-stacks back into concrete actions with visible actors, tuning the bridges between paragraphs, and cutting genuine wordiness. Prose revision changes how a fixed set of claims is rendered. It does not, by itself, rearrange the argument.

Keeping them separate matters because they answer to different tests and because mixing them hides mistakes. If a single pass both moves a section and rewords its sentences, and the result is worse, you cannot tell which move caused the harm. Run them as distinct, ordered activities.

## The supreme fidelity invariant

Both kinds of revision (and every other transformation the method allows, at every scale) are subordinate to one invariant. No change may alter the meaning of what is written. Concretely, all of the following must survive a revision unchanged:

- **Claims**: what the text actually asserts. A rewrite may not strengthen, weaken, or swap the proposition.
- **Evidence**: the facts, data, and support offered for those claims.
- **Numbers**: every quantity, exactly. "Roughly 40%" may not become "most," and "40%" may not become "over 40%."
- **Dates**: every time reference, exactly and with its original precision.
- **Names**: of people, places, products, parties. No silent substitution or generalization.
- **Negations**: whether something is affirmed or denied. Dropping or adding a "not" is a meaning change, and negation is easy to mangle when a sentence is restructured.
- **Modality and probability**: the difference between *will*, *may*, *must*, and *might*; between *certain*, *likely*, and *possible*. A hedge is content. "The change may reduce errors" must not become "the change reduces errors."
- **Conditions**: the *if* under which a claim holds. "Provided the load stays below capacity, the system is stable" must not lose its proviso.
- **Exceptions**: the carve-outs. "All accounts migrate, except legacy trials" must not drop the exception.
- **Qualifications**: the scope limits and caveats that fence a claim in. "In our sample" and "for enterprise customers" are not decoration.
- **Technical terminology**: the exact concept a term names. When plain wording replaces a technical term, the substitute must denote precisely the same thing, neither narrowed nor widened. Replacing "latency" with "speed" often widens; replacing "primate" with "monkey" narrows.
- **The writer's position**: the stance and voice the author has taken. Revision serves the writer's argument; it does not overwrite the writer's claim or commitments in the name of a stylistic rule.

The operative principle: **when a "clearer" rewrite would change any of these, the rewrite is rejected, no matter how much better it reads.** Clarity is not a license to edit meaning. A revision that improves flow at the cost of dropping a hedge has not made the writing clearer; it has made it say something the writer did not say. The fidelity check is the pass that confirms none of the items above moved: that parents still summarize their children faithfully, that reductions kept the real claim, and that rewrites held technical sense and who-did-what-to-whom intact.

*Worked example.* Original: "In the pilot region, the new routing may have cut delivery times, though we cannot rule out seasonal effects." A tempting rewrite: "The new routing cut delivery times." It is shorter and more forceful, and it is forbidden. It deletes the location qualifier ("in the pilot region"), promotes a hedge to a certainty ("may have" to did), and drops an explicit alternative explanation ("seasonal effects"). Three fidelity violations in nine words. The correct revision keeps all three while improving only what can be improved without touching them: for instance, tightening "we cannot rule out" if and only if the replacement preserves the same uncertainty.

## The same-passage conflict escalates; it does not auto-decide

Most of the time, structural needs and prose needs cooperate, or a little ingenuity satisfies both: a change of voice, a fronted phrase, a sentence split in two, a keyword echoed across a boundary. Before treating anything as a conflict, exhaust these reconciling rewrites; most apparent conflicts dissolve here.

But a genuine case can remain: within the *same passage*, the arrangement the argument's logic requires is, faithfully rendered, harder to read than an alternative arrangement that would disturb that logic, and no rewrite reconciles them. What then?

This method deliberately refuses to install a default winner. There is no standing rule that structure beats prose, and none that prose beats structure. Two things are firm, and only two:

1. **Reader comprehension is the supreme criterion.** When two arrangements both preserve meaning, the one the intended reader can understand with less effort wins, regardless of which layer proposed it. An arrangement that *misstates* the logic loses, but it loses under fidelity, because a garbled logical relation is itself a meaning change, not because "structure wins."

2. **A genuine, unresolved same-passage conflict is escalated, not decided by fiat.** Where an arrangement is faithful yet genuinely harder to read, and no reconciling rewrite exists, the passage is flagged and sent back for re-planning or a human ruling. The method does not silently keep the structural version, and it does not silently keep the prose version, and it never averages them into a mush that serves neither. It records the tie as open.

This is a real limit, stated honestly rather than papered over. Inventing a substantive default here would be inventing an answer the underlying principles do not supply. Escalation is the correct behavior, not a failure of nerve.

Fidelity sits above even this. If the only way to satisfy both layers would alter meaning, neither layer wins and the passage is escalated, never quietly reworded into something the writer did not mean.

## Comparing an original with a revision

Because meaning can slip without anyone intending it, a revision is not trusted until it has been checked against what it replaced. The comparison has a specific job: not to admire the improvement, but to catch what the improvement may have cost.

Classify every change as either meaning-preserving or meaning-altering. A meaning-altering change is never reported as a style win; it is reported as a fidelity problem, whatever else it did for the prose. Then, for the preserving changes, judge whether they actually reduce reader effort, tying each verdict to a concrete reader effect, not to taste.

The comparison hunts two failure modes in particular:

- **Dropped qualifications.** Walk the original for every hedge, scope limit, condition, exception, and number, and confirm each one survives in the revision, with its original force and precision. A revision that reads more confidently *because it quietly deleted the caveats* has failed, and the comparison exists to expose exactly that. List each dropped qualification with where it was in the original.

- **Added claims.** Confirm the revision asserts nothing the original did not. A rewrite can smuggle in a proposition (an implied cause, a stronger generalization, an unearned conclusion) under cover of "just tidying." Any claim present in the revision but absent from the original is flagged, not accepted.

The default posture of the comparison is skeptical: do not assume every change is an improvement, and do not let fluency vouch for fidelity. The most dangerous revisions are the ones that read beautifully and mean something slightly different, because those are the ones a casual reader will wave through. The comparison is the safeguard against precisely the change that looks like a win and is actually a loss.
