# Usage Judgment

Sooner or later every writer hits a contested usage question. Can a sentence begin with
*however*? Is it wrong to end with a preposition? Does *data* take a singular or plural
verb? Is *hopefully* allowed to mean "it is to be hoped"? The internet, the style guides,
and the confident colleague down the hall will each give you a different flat answer, and
most of those answers are stated as if handed down from a mountain.

This chapter is not a list of rulings. It is a **procedure for reaching your own**, one
question at a time. The premise is that usage disputes are settled by evidence, by the
practice of careful writers and readers, by register, and by effect on the reader, not
by decree, tradition, or the loudest authority. Because the inputs are evidence and
judgment rather than a lookup table, this is explicitly an area of **reasoned judgment**.
Treat it as a place to think, not a place to run a rule automatically. Where an automated
system touches usage, it should flag these calls for judgment rather than "correct" them
deterministically.

---

## 1. The stance: descriptive foundation, pragmatic overlay

Two ideas sit under everything below, and they can seem to pull against each other until
you see how they fit.

**Descriptive foundation.** What is correct in a language is, at bottom, what its
community of careful users actually does and accepts. There is no separate authority above
usage that usage must answer to. Rules are generalizations about practice, and practice
changes, so the rules change with it. A construction that a competent, careful writer uses
without anyone noticing is, by that fact, standard, regardless of what a hundred-year-old
handbook says.

**Pragmatic overlay.** You are not writing to be theoretically correct; you are writing to
reach a particular reader without friction. So even a "rule" you know to be a myth can be
worth honoring in a given piece, if breaking it would trip or distract the reader who
believes in it. Correctness in the abstract yields to communication in the concrete.

Put together: **decide what is genuinely standard by looking at evidence and careful
practice, then decide what to actually write by asking what serves this reader in this
setting.** The first question is descriptive; the second is pragmatic. Most bad usage
advice comes from collapsing the two: either treating a personal peeve as a law of the
language, or treating "technically defensible" as "therefore fine to use here."

**A caution about decline.** Running through much usage advice is the feeling that
the language is decaying: that standards are slipping and today's writing is worse
than some earlier golden age. Treat that feeling as a mood, not as evidence. Every
generation has voiced it about the one that followed; alarms were once raised about
constructions now considered impeccable; and the sense of decline has never tracked
any actual loss of expressive power. A change in usage is not, by itself, a
degradation. When the decline reflex is what is driving a verdict, discount it: it
is a reliable engine of folklore, and it will push you to enforce as a rule whatever
happened to be current when you first learned to write.

## 2. The judgment procedure

When a usage question arises, work through these steps rather than reaching for a verdict.

### Step 1: Identify what kind of question it is

Sort the issue into one of these, because they have different tests:

- **A genuine grammatical constraint**: a rule that, if broken, produces something no
  fluent reader would accept, or that creates real ambiguity about meaning.
- **A register or formality matter**: a choice that is fine in one setting and wrong in
  another, not right or wrong in itself.
- **A contested "rule" that may be a myth**: a prohibition asserted by tradition that may
  not reflect careful practice at all.

Misclassifying is the most common error. People defend register preferences as if they
were grammar, and dismiss real ambiguities as mere fussiness. Name the category first.

### Step 2: Consult the evidence and the practice

For the contested cases, ask what careful writers and readers actually do:

- Do respected, edited writers use the disputed form without apology?
- Does the form appear naturally in the kind of writing you are producing?
- Is there a real distinction the form preserves, or a real confusion it causes?

Evidence outranks assertion. That a rule is old, or widely repeated, or emphatically
stated, is not evidence that it reflects the language. Many of the most-repeated
prohibitions were invented by individuals and never described how the language works.

### Step 3: Separate grammar from register

If the form is grammatical but merely informal, the question is not "is this correct?" but
"is this correct *here*?" A contraction, a sentence-final preposition, a *sentence* that
starts with *and*: these are ordinary in most prose and out of place in the most formal
registers. Match the choice to the setting and the reader's expectations, and stop calling
it a matter of correctness. Grammar tells you what the language permits; register tells you
what this occasion permits.

### Step 4: Check for a real reader cost on either side

Two distinct reader costs can decide the call:

- **Ambiguity or misreading**: the form genuinely makes the sentence harder to
  understand or lets it be read two ways. This is a strong reason to change it regardless
  of what any rule says, because it is a direct hit to comprehension.
- **Distraction from a believed rule**: the form is fine, but a meaningful share of your
  readers think it is an error, and their noticing it will pull them out of the sentence.
  This is the pragmatic reason to sometimes obey a rule you know to be a myth.

Weigh both. A form can be perfectly standard and still worth avoiding because it will
snag readers; a form can be technically frowned upon and still worth using because the
"correct" alternative is clumsy or unclear.

### Step 5: Decide, and note that it was a judgment

Make the call for this piece and this audience. The same question can resolve differently
in a text message, a product manual, and a legal brief, and that is not inconsistency: it
is the procedure working. Record, at least to yourself, that the decision was a judgment
about a reader, not the application of a fixed law. That framing keeps you from turning
your own resolution into the next dogma.

## 3. Distinguishing genuine rules from myths

Some constraints are load-bearing; break them and meaning collapses or the sentence stops
being English. Others are folklore. Two quick tests separate them.

**The comprehension test (for genuine rules).** Does breaking the supposed rule create
ambiguity, invert who did what, or produce a string no fluent reader would accept?
Subject-verb agreement, pronoun reference that stays clear, word order that keeps the
actor and the acted-upon straight: violations of these hurt understanding. They are real.

Take agreement: "The list of returned items *were* long" mismatches the verb to the true
subject (*list*, not *items*) and makes an attentive reader stumble to sort out what is
being described. That is a real fault because it costs comprehension.

**But mind the opposite error: notional agreement.** Agreement can legitimately
follow the *sense* of the subject, not only its surface form, and English lets a
writer pick number by construal. A collective noun taken as a single unit takes a
singular verb ("The committee *has* decided"); the same noun taken as its several
members takes a plural ("The committee *were* split over the vote"). In the same
way, a singular-but-generic antecedent routinely takes a plural pronoun ("Every
applicant should check *their* status"), and this is standard, not an error. So do
not "correct" a construal that is deliberate and clear. The agreement fault worth
flagging is the *accidental* mismatch, like the *list…were* case above where the
verb has simply latched onto the nearest noun, not a writer's considered choice to
treat a sense-plural collective as plural. Over-enforcing formal agreement against a
sound notional one is itself a small hypercorrection (Section 4).

**The provenance test (for myths).** Where did the prohibition come from, and does careful
practice honor it? A number of famous "rules" are known to have been asserted by
individuals reasoning from analogy to other languages or from personal preference, and to
have been ignored by excellent writers ever since. The ban on splitting an infinitive, the
ban on ending a sentence with a preposition, the ban on starting one with a conjunction:
each is repeated constantly and followed by almost no one whose prose you admire.

An example of the split-infinitive myth in action. "She decided to gradually reduce the
dose" places *gradually* exactly where it belongs, right against the verb it modifies.
Rearranging it to avoid splitting *to reduce*: "She decided to reduce the dose gradually"
is tolerable, but "She decided gradually to reduce the dose" now suggests the *deciding*
was gradual, introducing an ambiguity to satisfy a rule that protects nothing. Here the
myth actively degrades the sentence.

**A fuller keep-or-discard battery.** The two tests above resolve most cases, but
for a genuinely contested rule it helps to run a short checklist and weigh the
answers together. Lean toward *keeping* the rule to the extent that:

- **it marks a distinction that aids the reader**: following it preserves a
  difference in meaning the reader would otherwise lose;
- **careful writers observe it now**: in the edited prose of your own era, not
  only in a handbook from a century ago;
- **breaking it causes real ambiguity or misreading**, rather than merely
  offending a shibboleth that signals group membership while protecting no meaning;
- **it is a matter of grammar, not merely register**: a genuine constraint of the
  language, not just a mark of formality, which is a separate axis settled by
  matching the setting;
- **a reputable modern reference supports it**: a current usage guide, or a
  dictionary that records how careful writers actually use the term, not an
  amateur style sheet or a single peever;
- **following it does not force an awkward hypercorrection**: if obeying the rule
  reliably yields something stiffer or stranger than the plain version, that counts
  against the rule, not against the plain version.

No one answer settles it. A putative rule that fails most of these is folklore; one
that passes most is load-bearing; and the mixed cases are exactly where the
pragmatic overlay (Section 5) decides whether to honor it for a particular reader.

The lesson is not "ignore all traditional rules." It is: **know which is which.** Honor
the genuine ones always; honor the myths only when a specific reader makes it worth it
(see below).

## 4. Hypercorrection: the error that comes from trying too hard

A distinctive class of mistakes comes not from carelessness but from over-applying a
half-remembered rule. Hypercorrection is what happens when a writer, anxious to be
correct, produces something wrong.

The classic pattern is the pronoun "correction." Someone learns that "*me and him* went"
is wrong and over-generalizes to "*I* over *me* is more correct," yielding "between you and
*I*" or "she gave it to *he* and *I*." Both are wrong: after a preposition the objective
form is required, so it is "between you and *me*." The writer reached for the fancier-
looking pronoun precisely because they were trying to be correct, and overshot.

The same impulse drives writers to avoid perfectly good constructions: recasting a clean
sentence into something stilted to dodge a nonexistent rule, or swapping a plain word for
a formal one that does not fit. The tell is that the result sounds strained. **When a
"correct" version reads as more awkward than the version it replaced, suspect
hypercorrection and check whether the rule you are obeying is real.** Correctness that
makes the prose worse is usually not correctness at all.

## 5. When to follow a contested rule anyway

Here is where the pragmatic overlay earns its place. Suppose you have done the work and
concluded that some prohibition is a myth: the form you want to use is standard, careful
writers use it, it causes no ambiguity. You may *still* choose to obey the myth. The
reason is entirely about the reader.

If a noticeable portion of your audience believes the form is an error, then using it, even
correctly, spends their attention. They stop following your point and start judging your
grammar, or worse, conclude you do not know better. In a document whose job is to be
trusted or to be effortless, that flicker of distraction is a real cost, and it can be
larger than the cost of the small, safe workaround.

So the decision rule is: **when a contested form is genuinely fine but a meaningful share
of your readers think it is wrong, and a clean alternative exists, prefer the alternative, not because the rule is right, but because the distraction is expensive.** This is not
surrender to dogma; it is the same reader-first calculation the whole methodology runs on.
You are optimizing for the reader's smooth passage, and their beliefs about grammar are
part of the terrain, whether or not those beliefs are well-founded.

Two qualifications keep this from becoming an excuse to obey everything:

- **The alternative must not be worse.** If dodging the myth forces a clumsy or ambiguous
  sentence (as the split-infinitive dodge did above), the distraction of the myth is the
  lesser cost, and you use the natural form. You are trading costs, not automatically
  paying the traditionalists.
- **The audience actually has to hold the belief.** Do not contort your prose for an
  imagined army of purists. Weigh the real readership. Writing for linguists, or for a
  casual audience that has never heard of the rule, you simply use the natural form.

---

## 6. Why this stays a judgment, not a correction pass

Everything above resists being turned into an automatic filter, and that resistance is the
point. Whether a term is standard depends on evolving practice; whether a form fits depends
on register; whether to honor a myth depends on a live estimate of who is reading and what
they believe. None of those are lookup operations. They are judgments about a specific
reader in a specific setting, and they can flip from one document to the next without
anyone being inconsistent.

The practical consequence: usage should be handled as an **advisory, reasoned layer**, not
a deterministic one. A system or a checklist can surface a contested form and lay out the
considerations (is this genuine or myth, what register are we in, will this reader stumble), but the resolution belongs to judgment. Flag the call; do not silently "fix" it. A
confident auto-correction here will, often enough, enforce a myth, flatten a legitimate
register choice, or introduce a hypercorrection, precisely because it treats a judgment as
a rule.

Hold two things at once, then. Ground your sense of correctness in what careful users
actually do: that keeps you from enforcing folklore. And bend your actual choices toward
the reader in front of you: that keeps you from being right in a way that fails to
communicate. The competent writer is not the one who knows the most rules. It is the one
who knows which rules are real, which are myths, and when even a myth is worth honoring for
the sake of the person reading.
