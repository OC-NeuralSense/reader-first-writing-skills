# Word Choice and Concreteness

Word-level revision is where a sound argument either reaches the reader or stalls a
few inches short of them. The structure can be correct, the paragraph order can be
defensible, and the sentence can still land as fog because a single abstract noun
sits where an action should be, or because a term the writer has said a thousand
times means nothing to the person reading it once. This chapter treats the smallest
unit of the craft: the individual word and the phrase it sits in.

The organizing test underneath everything here is the same one that governs the rest
of the methodology: **does this reduce or increase the reader's work?** Word choice is
not a matter of taste or elegance for its own sake. A word earns its place by helping
the intended reader build the intended meaning with less effort. When two words carry
the same meaning, the one that costs the reader less wins. That is the whole standard,
and most of the specific guidance below is just that standard applied to a recurring
situation.

A note on the status of these rules. Some of what follows is a strong principle you
should treat as a default and depart from only with a reason. Some is an optional
heuristic that helps in common cases but has real exceptions. The text marks which is
which. Treat the concreteness test and the audience-calibration rule as strong; treat
the concision and passive-voice guidance as judgment calls bounded by the reader-work
standard, not as bans.

---

## 1. Concreteness and the "can the reader picture it?" test

### The principle

Prefer language the reader can perceive. Concrete nouns and specific verbs give the
mind something to build; abstract summary words give it a label to file and nothing to
see. The human mind is built to handle things it can picture, locate, and watch move.
Prose that traffics in the picturable is easier to follow not because it is simpler in
content but because it is loading meaning into the machinery the reader already runs
for free.

This is a **strong principle**. Default to the concrete. The test is direct: read a
sentence and ask whether a reader who did not already know your meaning could form a
mental image, or at least a definite instance, from your words. If the honest answer is
no, the words are carrying less than they appear to.

### What abstraction costs

Consider a sentence like:

> The initiative achieved meaningful improvements in operational outcomes across
> several dimensions of the customer relationship.

Every content word here is a container with nothing visible inside it. *Initiative*,
*improvements*, *outcomes*, *dimensions*, *relationship*: the reader can file each one
but cannot see any of it. Now the concrete version:

> After we let customers reschedule deliveries by text, fewer packages came back
> undelivered, and support calls about missed drop-offs dropped by half.

The second sentence is longer, and it is far easier to read, because the reader can
watch it happen: a text message, a package at a door, a phone that rings less. The
length went up and the reader's work went down. That trade (more words, less effort)
recurs throughout this chapter and is worth internalizing: **concision is not measured
in words; it is measured in reader work.**

### Using the test in revision

When you catch a sentence that resists a picture, you usually have one of three
situations:

- **The abstraction is hiding a concrete claim you could just make.** Replace the
  container with its contents. "Improvements in outcomes" becomes "fewer returned
  packages."
- **The abstraction is genuinely the right level** because you are summarizing many
  concrete instances on purpose (this is common and legitimate at the tops of an
  argument, where a parent statement stands in for its details). Here abstraction is
  doing real work; leave it, but make sure the details underneath it are concrete when
  the reader reaches them.
- **The abstraction is a stand-in for thinking you have not finished.** Vague wording
  often marks a spot where the writer has not yet decided exactly what they mean.
  Forcing the concrete version is a way of finishing the thought.

The test is not a demand that every sentence be a snapshot. Argument requires
generalization. The demand is that abstraction be a deliberate choice with concrete
support beneath it, not a default reached for because it sounds weightier.

## 2. Turning nominalizations back into actions

### The pattern

A nominalization is an action or a quality frozen into a noun. *Decide* becomes
*decision*, *fail* becomes *failure*, *apply* becomes *application*, *reluctant*
becomes *reluctance*. English lets you do this freely, and doing it drains the life out
of a sentence, because the verb (the word that shows something happening) gets
demoted to a lifeless placeholder like *make*, *conduct*, *perform*, or *is*.

Watch what happens to the action:

> The team conducted an evaluation of the vendor and reached a determination that a
> replacement was warranted.

The two real actions, *evaluate* and *determine*, have been turned into nouns
(*evaluation*, *determination*) and propped up by empty verbs (*conducted*, *reached*).
Restore the verbs:

> The team evaluated the vendor and decided to replace it.

Ten words instead of eighteen, and every remaining word does something. This is the
single highest-yield word-level move in most drafts.

### How to spot and fix it

Scan for nouns ending in *-tion*, *-ment*, *-ance*, *-ence*, *-al*, *-ity*, and for the
weak verbs that tend to sit beside them (*make*, *do*, *conduct*, *perform*, *achieve*,
*provide*, *undertake*, *is/are*). When you find the pair, ask who did what, and rebuild
the sentence around that answer with a live verb and a visible actor.

- *provide assistance to* → *help*
- *reach an agreement* → *agree*
- *there was a decline in enrollment* → *enrollment fell*
- *the implementation of the policy* → *implementing the policy* (or better, *when we
  applied the policy*)

This is a **strong default**, with two honest exceptions:

- **The nominalization is the established name of the thing.** In technical or legal
  writing, *the settlement*, *the deployment*, or *the injection* may be the precise
  term for a specific object or event, not an evasion. Naming a thing is not the same as
  dodging an action.
- **You deliberately want the action treated as a known topic** rather than as news.
  Turning an action into a noun lets you refer back to it as something already on the
  table ("That decision proved costly"). This is a legitimate cohesion move, covered
  more in the coherence material; the point here is only that de-nominalizing is a
  default you can override for a reason, not a law.

## 3. Calibrating jargon and abstraction to the audience

### The variable that decides

There is no fixed list of forbidden words. Whether a term is jargon depends entirely on
who is reading. *Idempotent* is precise and welcome among engineers and noise to a
general reader; *net promoter score* is a shared shorthand on a marketing team and a
riddle in a letter to customers. The deciding variable is the reader's mastered
vocabulary: the set of terms genuinely entrenched in the group you are writing for.

The rule: **leave undefined only the terms genuinely entrenched in your reader's
in-group; unpack or replace the rest.** This is a **strong principle**, and it comes
with a built-in bias, because writers are bad at judging it.

### The reason you will get it wrong in one direction

Expertise is corrosive to this judgment. Once a term is second nature to you, you can no
longer feel what it is like not to know it, and, worse, you cannot feel that you
cannot feel it. The gap is invisible from the inside. This is why the safe error is to
assume the reader knows slightly *less* than your gut says. When you are unsure whether
a term needs a gloss, it needs a gloss. Over-explaining costs an informed reader a
half-second of skimming; under-explaining costs an uninformed reader the sentence.

### Practical calibration moves

- **Define on first use, briefly, in-line.** A short gloss or a concrete example
  attached to a term the moment it appears is cheaper for the reader than a footnote or
  a glossary they have to leave the text to consult. "We used a *canary* (a small slice
  of live traffic routed to the new version first) to catch failures early."
- **Expand abbreviations the first time.** Spell out the full form and put the short
  form in parentheses, then use the short form thereafter. Never make the reader hold an
  unexpanded acronym in suspense.
- **Prefer the transparent label over the insider one** when both exist and both are
  accurate. If a plain term names the thing as precisely as the specialized one, the
  plain term is doing the same work at lower cost. Reserve the specialized term for when
  it carries a distinction the plain word would blur.
- **Do not confuse necessary abstraction with jargon.** Some content is abstract because
  the subject is abstract; some vocabulary is technical because the concept has no
  plainer name that keeps its exact meaning. Calibration means removing the terms that
  are gratuitous for your reader, not flattening genuine precision into approximate
  everyday words. When a plain substitution would narrow or widen the concept, keep the
  precise term and explain it.

The exception to plainness is a real one: for a genuine expert audience, entrenched
terms and a degree of abstraction are not merely tolerated, they are the efficient
choice. Spelling out what the reader already owns is its own kind of reader-work, and a
kind that mildly insults them. Calibration cuts both ways; the point is to match the
reader, not to minimize sophistication.

## 4. Concision without losing parsing cues

### The genuine tension

The advice to cut needless words is sound and widely known. Extra words that carry no
meaning tax the reader twice: once to read them and again to discover they added
nothing. Strip them.

But there is a countervailing truth that the "omit needless words" slogan hides: **some
words carry no meaning yet still earn their place by helping the reader parse the
sentence.** These are not content words; they are structural cues: a *that* that marks
where a clause begins, a *which* or a repeated preposition that keeps phrases from
colliding, a small function word that prevents the reader from briefly grabbing the
wrong reading and having to back up. Difficulty is a property of structure, not of word
count. A shorter sentence that forces a misstep is harder than a slightly longer one
that runs clean.

### How the tension resolves

It resolves on the reader-comprehension standard, the same one that governs everything
else: **cut a word if removing it costs the reader nothing; keep a word if removing it
makes the sentence briefly misparse, blurs a phrase boundary, or breaks the rhythm the
reader is using to chunk the sentence.**

A concrete case. Compare:

> The proposal the committee rejected last spring is back on the agenda.

with the version that keeps the optional *that*:

> The proposal that the committee rejected last spring is back on the agenda.

The first is shorter and reads fine, because *the committee* immediately signals that a
new clause has started. Now a case where dropping the marker hurts:

> The evidence the prosecutor knew was fabricated changed the verdict.

For a beat, the reader takes *the evidence the prosecutor knew* as a complete unit (the
evidence that the prosecutor was aware of) and then *was fabricated* forces a rewind.
Restoring the marker prevents the stumble:

> The evidence that the prosecutor knew was fabricated changed the verdict.

Same three deleted characters; opposite effect on reader work. The rule is not "always
keep *that*" or "always cut it." It is: **keep the words that protect the reader's first
pass through the sentence, and cut the rest.** Concision is bounded by parseability, not
by the word count.

### A working procedure

1. Delete every word that adds no meaning and whose removal leaves the sentence just as
   easy to parse: filler intensifiers (*very*, *really*, *quite*), redundant pairs
   (*each and every*), throat-clearing (*it is important to note that*), and empty
   qualifiers.
2. For each remaining candidate for deletion, read the sentence without it and check for
   a stumble, a misreading, or a lost boundary. If one appears, the word was structural;
   put it back.
3. Judge the result by how the sentence reads, not by how few words it has. The goal is
   the lightest sentence the reader can process in one pass, which is not always the
   shortest one on the page.

## 5. The passive voice as a tool, not a defect

### Reject the blanket ban

The most repeated piece of style advice (avoid the passive voice) is wrong as stated.
The passive is a construction, not an error. It lets you rearrange who appears where in
a sentence without changing what the sentence claims. That rearrangement is sometimes
exactly what the reader needs. The correct rule is **functional: judge each passive by
what it does for the reader, and keep or revert it on that basis.**

### When the passive is the right choice

- **To keep the topic in the subject slot.** Readers follow a passage most easily when
  each sentence starts from something already in play and moves to something new. If the
  thing you are continuing to talk about happens to be the target of the action rather
  than its doer, the passive lets you keep it up front. "The bridge opened in 1932. It
  was closed for repairs twice in the next decade, and was finally demolished in 1961."
  Forcing those into the active voice would keep swapping the subject and make the reader
  re-find the topic each time.
- **To background an agent the reader does not need, or that you genuinely do not know.**
  "The samples were refrigerated overnight." Who did it is irrelevant; naming a lab
  technician would only add a participant the reader must process and discard. Likewise,
  "The window had been forced open" is honest when nobody knows who did it. The passive
  here is not evasion; it is omitting a genuine irrelevance.
- **To manage sentence weight and flow**, placing a long or heavy phrase where it reads
  most easily rather than jamming it into the subject.

### When to revert it

Revert the passive when it hides an actor the reader actually needs. "The safety warning
was removed from the label" is the telling case: the construction is grammatical and
perfectly clear as a sentence, but it drops the one piece of information (who removed it)
that the context demands. The fault there is not the passive voice; it is the missing agent. The diagnosis
is always the same question: **does dropping or demoting the actor serve the reader, or
protect the writer?** If the reader needs to know who acted, name them, in whatever voice
puts that cleanly.

So the working stance is neither "avoid the passive" nor "use the passive freely." It is:
default to the active because it usually names the actor and reads as movement, and reach
for the passive deliberately when it improves topic continuity, drops a true irrelevance,
or balances the sentence, flagging for a second look any passive whose only effect is to
make an agent disappear.

## Summary of standing rules

- **Prefer the picturable** (strong). If the reader cannot form an image or a definite
  instance, the words are underperforming, unless you are deliberately summarizing, in
  which case keep the detail concrete underneath.
- **Restore live verbs from nominalizations** (strong default; exceptions for
  established names and deliberate topic-marking).
- **Calibrate vocabulary to the reader's mastered terms** (strong), biasing toward
  assuming slightly too little, because expertise hides the gap. Define on first use,
  expand abbreviations, prefer transparent labels, but keep genuine precision.
- **Cut words that cost the reader nothing; keep words that protect the first pass**
  (judgment, bounded by comprehension). Concision is reader-work, not word-count.
- **Use the passive by function** (judgment): keep it for topic continuity, irrelevant
  or unknown agents, and weight; revert it when it hides an actor the reader needs.

Every one of these reduces to the single question the whole methodology turns on: which
choice lets this reader build this meaning with less work?
