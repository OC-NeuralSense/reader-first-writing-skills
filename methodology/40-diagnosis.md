# Diagnosing a Draft

Diagnosis is the act of finding what is wrong with a piece of writing and saying so precisely. It is not the act of fixing anything. Keeping those two acts apart is the first discipline of this method: a diagnosis produces a report, not a rewrite. The moment a diagnostic pass starts silently repairing what it finds, it stops being a diagnosis and becomes an unaccountable edit, and the writer loses the chance to see the pattern of faults, weigh them, and decide what to do.

This chapter covers how to run a diagnosis, how to keep two very different kinds of fault on separate layers, and how to turn a pile of findings into a decision about what to repair first.

## Two layers, never merged

Every fault a draft can have belongs to one of two layers, and the single most common diagnostic mistake is to confuse them.

**The structural layer** is about the architecture of the whole: what question the document exists to answer, how its claims are grouped, what order those groups sit in, and whether each higher-level statement is honestly earned by the material beneath it. Structural faults are faults of arrangement and logic. You could fix every sentence in the document and still leave them all in place.

**The prose layer** is about the sentences and paragraphs themselves: whether a sentence can be parsed on the first pass, whether the reader can recover the connection between one sentence and the next, whether references are unambiguous, whether wording is concrete, and whether the text is bloated or thin. Prose faults are faults of surface rendering. You could have a flawless argument and still bury it in unreadable sentences.

Report the two layers separately. A finding on one layer should never be listed alongside a finding on the other as if they were the same kind of problem, because they call for different remedies, sit at different scales, and are often fixed by different people at different times. A single merged list of "issues" hides the one distinction that most determines what to do next.

### Structural faults to look for

A structural diagnosis audits the argument as an object, independent of how well or badly it is written. The recurring faults:

- **No governing question, or the wrong one.** A document should exist to answer one reducible question. If you cannot state that question in a single line, or if the draft is quietly answering a different question than the one its reader actually carries, everything below inherits the defect. *Example:* a memo titled "Warehouse Throughput" reads as though it is answering "how does our warehouse work?" when the reader (a regional manager deciding on overtime) is really asking "can we clear the backlog before quarter-end without hiring?" The draft answers the wrong question competently.

- **Ungrouped or empty groupings.** Claims that are simply listed with no organizing logic, or headings that name a bucket without asserting anything. A section called "Considerations" that collects five unrelated points is an ungrouped grouping. A parent line that reads "There are three factors" is empty: it counts its children instead of summarizing them.

- **A buried answer.** The document's actual conclusion arrives on page three, after a long approach march, in a genre and for a reader who needed it first. The answer exists; it is just in the wrong place.

- **Mixed ordering.** Within one group, the members are sequenced by no consistent principle (part chronology, part importance, part whatever came to mind), so the reader cannot predict what comes next or why these things belong together.

- **An unsupported parent.** A summarizing statement that its children do not actually establish. The heading claims "the pilot succeeded on every metric," but the points beneath it cover two metrics and dodge a third. The parent overreaches its evidence.

### Prose faults to look for

A prose diagnosis audits the sentences as an object, independent of whether the argument behind them is sound. The recurring faults:

- **Incoherence.** The reader cannot recover the intended relation between adjacent sentences: is the second one an example of the first, a cause, a contrast, an exception? The link is in the writer's head but not on the page.

- **Heavy sentences.** Structures that stall the reader mid-parse: a long subject that keeps the verb waiting, a clause folded inside another clause inside another, or an opening that leads the reader down a false path they must back out of.

- **Ambiguity.** A pronoun with two possible antecedents, a modifier that could attach to either of two things, a phrase that reads two ways. *Example:* "The auditor told the director she had missed the deadline": who missed it?

- **Abstraction.** Wording so removed from anything picturable that the reader cannot form a mental image and so cannot check their understanding. Stacks of nouns naming processes and capabilities, with no visible actor doing anything.

- **Wordiness.** Text carrying words that add neither meaning nor parsing help. Note the qualifier: not all extra words are waste: some mark a phrase boundary or block a misreading and must stay. Wordiness is only the surplus that costs the reader effort for nothing in return.

## Diagnosis reports; it does not rewrite

Each finding should carry two things: a location, and the specific test it failed. "This is unclear" is not a diagnosis; it is a complaint. "The pronoun in the second sentence of paragraph four has two possible antecedents (the auditor and the director) so the reader cannot tell who missed the deadline" is a diagnosis: it points to a place and names the concrete failure.

The reason to hold back from fixing is not caution for its own sake. It is that the fix is a separate decision with its own constraints (above all the requirement to preserve meaning), and that decision deserves to be made deliberately, not folded invisibly into the act of noticing. A diagnostic pass that also silently edits gives the writer a changed document and no map of what changed or why. Separating the two lets the writer see the whole shape of the trouble before committing to any repair.

One consequence: a diagnostic finding can be *wrong* about the remedy and still be a good finding. Its job is to identify and locate the fault accurately. Choosing the remedy comes next, and belongs to a different pass.

## Reading difficulty as evidence about both layers

A subtle point ties the two layers back together. When an outside reader stumbles at a particular sentence, the surface symptom is a prose fault, but the *cause* may be structural. A paragraph that will not cohere no matter how you tune its sentences is often a paragraph whose underlying claims do not actually belong together, or between which a necessary link is missing from the argument itself.

So treat persistent local incoherence as a diagnostic signal that points at both layers at once. Before concluding "these sentences need work," ask whether the sentences are fighting a structural gap. If they are, the finding belongs on the structural layer even though it first showed up as a prose symptom. This is exactly the kind of case where a merged issue-list would mislead: it would file a structural fault under prose and send it to the wrong repair.

## The self-review blind spot

The person who wrote the draft is the worst-placed person to diagnose it, and predictably so. Expertise in the material makes it nearly impossible to feel what a reader who lacks that expertise does not know. Worse, this gap conceals itself: the writer's own estimate of what is obvious runs high, and nothing in the writer's experience of the text reveals the miss. The sentence that is transparent to its author because the author already holds the missing piece reads as a wall to the reader who does not.

Two partial correctives. First, cooling delay: setting a draft aside long enough that you return to it closer to a stranger's footing, able to notice where you assumed too much. Second, and stronger, an actual outside reader whose stumbles are data. Neither corrective is perfect, but both beat re-reading your own work an hour after writing it and finding it clear.

## Prioritizing findings

A raw fault list is not yet actionable. Order it.

1. **Fidelity and soundness risks first.** Anything suggesting the document says something false, unsupported, or self-contradicting outranks anything about readability. A clear sentence that states an unproven claim is worse than a clumsy sentence that states a true one.

2. **Structural faults before prose faults, as a rule of sequence, not of rank.** This is a cost argument, not a claim that structure matters more. Repairing structure often moves, merges, or deletes whole passages; polishing a sentence that a later restructuring will delete is wasted work. Fixing the arrangement first means the prose pass operates on text that will actually survive. Both layers ultimately serve the same master (the reader's ease of understanding), and neither holds standing precedence over the other on the merits.

3. **Within a layer, order by reach.** A fault at the top of the argument (wrong governing question) contaminates everything beneath it; fix it before fixing a single mis-grouped subsection. A pervasive prose habit (chronic abstraction across the whole draft) matters more than one awkward sentence.

4. **Separate blocking from non-blocking.** Some faults must be gone before the document can be released; others are improvements that can wait. Say which is which. Not every finding is a ship-stopper, and treating them all as equally urgent buries the ones that are.

## Choosing a remediation path

Diagnosis ends by pointing at a path, not by walking it. The central routing decision is **restructure versus revise-prose**, and getting it right is one of the highest-value judgments in the whole method, because sending a structural fault to a prose fix (or the reverse) wastes effort and usually fails.

- If the findings cluster on the structural layer (wrong question, bad grouping, buried answer, unsupported parent), the path is structural revision, and prose polishing should wait until the architecture is settled.
- If the argument is sound and well-arranged but the sentences obstruct it, the path is prose revision.
- If a prose symptom turns out to be a structural gap in disguise (the incoherence-that-won't-fix case above), route it to structural revision despite its surface appearance.
- If findings are genuinely split across both layers, do the structural pass first for the sequencing reason above, then re-diagnose the surviving prose, because restructuring changes what prose even remains to fix.

Whatever the path, the diagnosis hands it off as a recommendation with reasons attached, and the repair (with its own preservation constraints) begins as a fresh, deliberate act.
